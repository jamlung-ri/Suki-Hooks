## 02. What "Real AI" in the EHR Looks Like

For sophisticated buyers, the first filter is simple: what counts as true AI, and what is just automation with a new label?

Christy Limbers describes how FMOL Health separates signal from noise:

- Machine learning that continuously improves based on real-world use
- Natural language processing that can interpret clinical conversations and free-text input
- Predictive and reasoning capabilities that support better decisions, not just faster clicks
- A tangible reduction in cognitive burden, not just more structured fields

With Suki, FMOL Health deploys an ambient assistant that:

- Listens to the patient-provider conversation
- Generates a structured note with measurable, actionable data
- Surfaces opportunities to improve documentation and coding

That combination of intelligence plus workflow impact is the line between "AI-washed" and truly ambient clinical intelligence.

> "We're an Epic shop and we leverage nearly every tool they offer. That doesn't automatically make it AI. For us, real AI combines machine learning, natural language processing, and predictive analytics in a way that reduces cognitive burden and improves outcomes. Otherwise, it's just workflow automation."
>
> — Christy Limbers

## 03. Built-In vs Best-of-Breed: How to Be Deliberate

When Epic and other EHRs announced their own ambient offerings, many leaders asked a blunt question: "If my EHR has an ambient tool, how do independent companies survive?"

Roy's answer is equally blunt: you have to be deliberate.

> "People think I'm anti-Epic. I'm not. Sometimes Epic is absolutely the right answer. But it is always a cost-benefit analysis. Best-in-class AI has to acknowledge the real costs of integration and complexity and still show that the benefit is materially above the alternative."
>
> — Roy Rosin

Three realities to consider:

1. **Good enough really is good enough… sometimes.** In low-stakes areas, the embedded EHR option will win by default. It is already there, already procured, and often "fine."

2. **In high-impact areas, "fine" is expensive.** If a third-party solution can add $10 million in revenue or savings where the EHR adds $10,000, the math is obvious. The burden is on the independent vendor to prove it with outcomes, not feature lists.

3. **AI is changing faster than EHRs can ship.** As Christy put it, AI is a rapidly changing landscape. Large EHRs are not rapidly changing software organizations. For technology that directly affects cognitive burden, retention, and revenue, waiting several years for embedded functionality to mature may be a strategic risk.

> "We can't afford to wait for a big EHR to eventually make the right move. Our physicians need relief now. That's why we deliberately partnered with Suki and treat them as a partner, not a vendor."
>
> — Christy Limbers

The question is not "EHR vs third party." The question is: **Where do we accept default tools, and where do we invest in excellence, because the upside is that big?**

## 05. Outcomes, ROI, and the Logic Model for Value

Everyone talks about ROI. Fewer teams can explain their logic model.

> "Metrics like 'documentation time' or 'pajama time' are not the same thing. You have to be precise about what matters and set targets, not just metrics. Otherwise, you get fuzzy attribution and disappointment."
>
> — Roy Rosin

### Step 1. Decide what kind of ROI you want.

**Provider experience and retention**
- Reduce after-hours work and pajama time
- Lower cognitive load
- Increase perceived presence with patients

**Financial performance**
- Faster note closure and earlier bill drop
- More complete documentation and defensible coding
- Reduced denials and rework in the mid-revenue cycle

**Patient experience and outcomes**
- Better perception of time and attention from clinicians
- Fewer delays caused by documentation and prior authorizations

> "We were absolutely not trying to squeeze more patients into already burned-out clinicians' days. Our primary goal was reducing cognitive burden and improving the quality and efficiency of documentation. Financial ROI came from that, not from forcing higher volumes."
>
> — Christy Limbers

Christy outlined how FMOL Health approached Suki. FMOL Health measured:

- **Note closure time:** They saw an 84% improvement in notes closed within seven days, which directly accelerated revenue recognition.
- **Documentation completeness:** More consistent capture of procedures and chronic conditions led to cleaner coding. As Christy put it, "You can't code what you don't remember."
- **Provider experience:** Reduced after-hours work and better alignment with what clinicians said they needed.

### Step 2. Build measurement systems, not just metrics.

Many health systems discover that key data is incomplete or fragmented. When that happens, the right move is to build the measurement capability alongside the implementation, rather than accepting "we can't measure it."

Signs of a mature measurement approach:

- **Set clear targets** — not just baselines
- **Use a mix of process metrics and outcome metrics** — adoption, note closure, and error rates are examples of process metrics; revenue uplift, denials, turnover, and burnout indicators are examples of outcome metrics
- **Develop a shared view of what counts as success** — incorporating both vendor input and health system input

> "I'm seeing more CFOs bake vendor projections directly into their P&L. If you say you're going to save $3 million, they will hold you to $3 million. That kind of accountability is healthy."
>
> — Roy Rosin

### Red flags that value is more hype than reality

1. The vendor cannot articulate how they get to the ROI, only the headline number
2. No credible way to measure the promised outcomes using your actual data
3. A focus on features and screenshots instead of measurable change in workflow and results
4. No answer to "what happens if we don't hit the target, and how will we course-correct?"

## 06. Risk, Defensibility, and Medical-Legal Reality

As AI moves closer to the heart of clinical and revenue workflows, questions about risk and defensibility are unavoidable.

Two principles emerged repeatedly in the discussion:

### 01. Defensible optimization, not blind optimization

> "The best teams know the difference between optimization and defensible optimization. They study every rejection, denial, and complaint to learn where the line is."
>
> — Roy Rosin

In practical terms, that looks like:

- Training models on clean, defensible documentation, not just historical patterns that may include risky behavior
- Studying the root cause of denials, payer disputes, and medical-legal issues and updating patterns accordingly
- Building explicit boundaries into the system so it does not suggest patterns that cross those lines

### 02. Clinician-in-the-loop and full traceability

In practical terms, that looks like:

- **Grounded suggestions.** Coding recommendations are supported by the transcript and EHR data through bidirectional integration, never hallucinated.
- **Clinicians and coders remain in control.** Suki recommends; humans decide. The AI does not silently submit codes or change the record.
- **Audit trails.** You can trace back where a suggestion came from, what evidence supported it, and what end-user changes were made.

> "We never want AI to be the decision maker. We want it to provide guidance and save time, while the clinician or coder remains the final authority. That is how you stay on the right side of both ethics and medical-legal reality."
>
> — Josh Margulies

## 07. Contracting in a Market That Changes Every Six Months

Traditional enterprise contracting assumed three to five-year commitments. In AI, that horizon is shrinking.

> "We are no longer making the long-term bets we used to. Instead of three to five-year contracts, we are looking at one-year agreements with no-harm out clauses. If a partner cannot meaningfully achieve the outcomes we agreed on, we need the ability to move on."
>
> — Roy Rosin

For health systems, shorter contracts create options, but they also raise a new question: **How do you build real value when switching costs are lower than ever?**

Roy's answer is to flip the frame. Instead of relying on artificial switching costs, the strongest vendors build **earned switching costs** through performance and acceleration.

> "You would think choosing a vendor is about who is better today. But that's only half the story. You also need to look at the slope of improvement. A team that is improving quickly will cross and surpass a stagnant competitor in short order."
>
> — Josh Margulies

In other words:

- Look at where the product is now, and how much it has improved in the last 6–12 months.
- Ask how often the vendor ships improvements, and how those improvements are driven by clinician feedback.
- Look for patterns where the system learns more about your workflows, documentation patterns, and revenue levers over time, making it more painful to walk away because it has become genuinely better for your organization.

That is how you create switching costs by delivering value, not by trapping customers.

## 08. What to Look For in an AI Assistant Partner

Based on the discussion and Suki's experience across health systems and partners, sophisticated buyers can use the following checklist when evaluating ambient and AI assistant solutions.

### Strategic criteria

**EHR-agnostic but EHR-deep.**
Bidirectional integration with major EHRs (Epic, Oracle Health, MEDITECH, athenahealth), not just superficial data export.

**Device and setting flexibility.**
Works across iOS, Android, web, and in multiple settings: ambulatory, inpatient, ED, urgent care, telehealth.

**Ecosystem strength.**
Used not only by clinicians directly, but also embedded in partner platforms that extend reach and data diversity.

### Clinical and workflow criteria

**True ambient plus assistance.**
Goes beyond transcription to support visit prep, documentation, orders, and assisted coding.

**Specialty-specific nuance.**
Able to adapt to neurology, oncology, GI, derm, and others instead of forcing a single generic template.

**Shoulder-to-shoulder change management.**
Willing to be on site, listening to clinicians and iterating rapidly when workflows break.

### Measurement and ROI criteria

**Clearly defined logic models for how AI changes:**
- Note closure time
- De-duplicated effort (pre-charting, chart review)
- Documentation completeness and coding accuracy
- Denials and rework

**Agreement up front on targets, not just metrics.**

**Willingness to be held accountable for projections and adjust if you are off-track.**

### Governance and risk criteria

- Clinician-in-the-loop by design
- Specialty-specific nuance
- Shoulder-to-shoulder change management

> "If the system doesn't work for their real workflow, and if you can't fix it quickly and prove you are listening, clinicians will walk away. They will go back to what they know, even if it's painful."
>
> — Christy Limbers
