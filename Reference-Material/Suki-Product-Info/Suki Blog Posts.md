## Source 4: AI That Listens: Why Context Matters More Than Accuracy in Clinical AI

**URL:** https://www.suki.ai/blog/ai-that-listens-why-context-matters-more-than-accuracy-in-clinical-ai/
**Published:** November 24, 2025
**Publisher:** Suki AI Blog

This blog post examines one of the central tensions in clinical AI: the difference between *accuracy* and *contextual intelligence*. The article opens with a clinical analogy — a high fever means something entirely different in a recently vaccinated child versus an elderly patient on chemotherapy. A creatinine of 250 is alarming for some patients and reassuring for others whose baseline is chronically elevated. These nuances guide clinical judgment as surely as any lab result.

> "The map gives us structure; the territory gives us truth."

**On accuracy in AI:**
Accuracy is the foundational requirement for trust and safety in clinical AI. If a model mistranscribes a consultation, misclassifies a lab result, or fails to detect an arrhythmia, it cannot credibly be used in practice. In healthcare AI, accuracy refers to how well a model's output aligns with a defined ground truth — the correct diagnosis, the correct transcription, the correct detection of an abnormality.

**The limits of accuracy alone:**
Reviews of AI in critical care show that while diagnostic accuracy continues to improve, demonstrable outcome gains remain limited. The Peterson Health Technology Institute noted that while ambient AI scribes show promise, "evidence demonstrating time savings or improved clinical efficiency remains limited." A model may hit 95% accuracy on a controlled dataset, but the incremental value of going from 95% to 97% flattens if the model lacks a deeper understanding of *why* the data looks the way it does.

**What context includes in healthcare:**
- A patient's baseline physiology and comorbidities
- Patterns of change over time
- Clinician workflow and health-plan coverage rules
- Conversational nuance
- Social and behavioral dynamics

Many of the limits observed in today's AI systems — the plateauing of performance despite larger datasets — stem from this lack of contextual understanding. Models can mirror patterns but not necessarily understand them. They know what the words are, but not what the words mean.

> "Accuracy tells us where we are on the map; context reminds us where we stand in the world."

## Source 6: From Notes to Narratives: Why the Future of Clinical AI Depends on Storytelling

**URL:** https://www.suki.ai/blog/from-notes-to-narratives-why-the-future-of-clinical-ai-depends-on-storytelling/
**Publisher:** Suki AI Blog

This post argues that clinical documentation is fundamentally an act of *storytelling*, and that the future of clinical AI depends on systems that understand narrative — not just data.

**The core thesis:**
Storytelling is how humans compress complexity, decide what matters, and coordinate action under uncertainty. Nowhere is dependence on shared narrative more constrained — or more consequential — than in medicine. Clinical documentation is not simply a record of what happened; it is an act of translation. When clinicians write notes, they are not enumerating facts but constructing a story that allows another human being (often someone they've never met) to understand what happened, why it matters, and what should happen next.

> "When clinicians write notes, they intentionally perform lossy compression: distilling a complex, high-dimensional reality into a narrative that preserves what matters for future decision-making."

**What AI systems currently do well:**
Modern AI systems, particularly large language models, have become increasingly good at capturing the *surface form* of clinical documentation. Transcription accuracy, factual correctness, and hallucination detection are real advances and essential foundations. Without fidelity, no meaningful narrative can exist.

**What fidelity alone cannot guarantee:**
A transcript can be accurate and still fail to preserve the clinical story. A note can be complete and yet obscure what matters. A fluent summary can flatten uncertainty, subtly changing how downstream clinicians interpret risk or urgency.

**Narrative devices that matter in clinical AI:**
- **Temporality:** Clinical reasoning is inherently narrative — illness unfolds in timelines, not tables. A fever starting three hours ago means something different from one smoldering for weeks.
- **Causality:** Clinical notes encode clinical reasoning through words like *given*, *because*, *likely*, *concerning for*. These are not stylistic flourishes; they encode the clinician's reasoning.
- **Emphasis:** Guides attention when time is short and stakes are high.
- **Omission:** Reflects judgment; benign findings are often left unstated because they are unremarkable.
- **Point of view:** Shifts depending on whether the note is written for handover, consultation, or medico-legal record.
- **Uncertainty:** Far from being a flaw, it is a signal that helps downstream clinicians calibrate vigilance and risk.

The clinical record is not a passive archive. It is active infrastructure — a thread that connects one decision to the next. Its primary function is not to store data, but to preserve meaning across time, people, and uncertainty. To build AI that truly supports clinicians, systems must encode not only the measurable but the meaningful.

## Source 13: Wide Adoption of Ambient Problem-Based Charting Across 50+ Specialties

**URL:** https://www.suki.ai/blog/wide-adoption-of-ambient-problem-based-charting/
**Published:** September 9, 2024
**Publisher:** Suki AI Blog

Problem-based charting (PBC), also called problem-oriented charting, is a common way clinicians document patient care in EHR systems. By organizing patient data around specific diagnoses or problems, PBC zeroes in on what truly matters in a patient's healthcare journey.

**Suki's ambient PBC** incorporates deep EHR integrations and cutting-edge AI to ensure the most important patient issues are front and center. Since its launch, adoption has spread across **50+ specialties** including primary care and sub-specialties such as cardiology, orthopedics, and psychiatry — across Epic, athenahealth, Oracle Cerner, and MEDITECH.

**How ambient PBC works:**
1. Suki recognizes problems discussed during the visit and generates problem-based plan suggestions.
2. Suki automatically pulls any pre-charted diagnoses from the EHR and suggests a plan for those discussed during the encounter.
3. If additional problems arise, Suki captures and suggests plans for those as well.
4. Clinicians have full control — they can accept, edit, or reject suggestions and prioritize problems in their preferred order.
5. Clinicians can also select ICD-10 and HCC codes for new problems.
6. When the note is submitted, new problems and their plans are added directly to the appropriate location in the EHR — both to the note and the encounter diagnosis list — no copy-pasting required.

**Clinical impact:**
- Streamlined documentation and workflows
- Reduced redundancy in documentation
- Improved data accuracy and better communication among healthcare providers
- More complete documentation with easier coding associated with each problem

## Source 14: Scaling Suki's Command Understanding: Building Fast and Accurate Intent Classification for Clinical Voice Assistants

**URL:** https://www.suki.ai/blog/scaling-sukis-command-understanding-building-fast-and-accurate-intent-classification-for-clinical-voice-assistants/
**Publisher:** Suki AI Blog (Engineering)

This technical post details how Suki built a new intent classification and slot-filling system that enables Suki to understand natural clinical commands with high accuracy while maintaining **sub-300ms response times** — all while working with limited training data.

**The challenge:**
As doctors increasingly rely on voice commands to interact with patient data — scheduling an X-ray, adding a diagnosis, querying patient information — the need for fast, accurate command interpretation becomes critical. Clinical voice commands present unique challenges:

- **Limited training data:** Clinical voice commands don't have large public datasets. Medical terminology and clinical workflows require working with proprietary data.
- **Speed requirements:** Clinical workflows demand real-time responses. LLM API calls introduce latency that disrupts physician focus on patient care.
- **Natural language variability:** Doctors don't speak in structured commands. As Suki expanded its intent fleet to complex commands like Q&A from patient data and natural language editing (NLEditing), doctors expect increasingly natural interactions.

**The solution — a dual-component system:**
Rather than relying on traditional classification approaches, Suki built a **retrieval-based system** using sentence encoders and vector similarity.

- **Data augmentation:** Used LLMs to generate synthetic training data, creating variations of clinical commands while maintaining semantic accuracy.
- **Vector Store Approach:** Trained a sentence encoder to create embeddings for all training queries, building a vector store that maps query embeddings to their ground truth intents. During inference, the system encodes the new query and uses k-Nearest Neighbor (kNN) voting.

This system represents a foundation for more advanced clinical voice understanding capabilities. As Suki continues expanding Suki Assistant's skill set — particularly in clinical Q&A and natural language editing — having fast, accurate intent classification becomes even more critical.

> "Building technology that truly assists clinicians requires solving complex technical challenges while never losing sight of the ultimate goal: helping doctors focus on what matters most, their patients."

**Abstract / Summary:**
This editorial explores the transformative potential of artificial intelligence (AI) in revolutionizing nursing documentation processes. **Core content:**

Nursing documentation stands as a critical aspect of healthcare delivery, ensuring comprehensive patient records and facilitating communication among healthcare providers. However, traditional documentation methods are often time-consuming and prone to errors, diverting nurses' attention from direct patient care.

Nurses spend a significant portion of their time meticulously documenting patient information — from vital signs to treatment plans and medical histories. This process, while crucial for maintaining accurate records, often diverts nurses' attention away from direct patient care.

**AI-powered approaches in nursing documentation:**

Integrating AI into nursing documentation offers numerous benefits but also presents significant ethical and legal challenges. Nurses must navigate these complexities to ensure that AI is implemented responsibly, prioritizing patient privacy, fairness, accountability, and professional autonomy.
